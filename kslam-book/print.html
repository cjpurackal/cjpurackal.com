Introduction

A textbook on visual and visual-inertial SLAM that derives each idea, draws it, and then reads it in working Rust.

This book teaches simultaneous localization and mapping from first principles. Every chapter develops the geometry and estimation behind one part of a SLAM system, then follows it into kornia-slam, a modular SLAM library built on kornia-rs.

Goals

This book has three goals:

  1. Understand SLAM. If you are new to it, learn what it is and how it works. If you already know it, find the gaps in what you know.
  2. Build deep intuition. Develop a mathematical feel for the ideas SLAM rests on, so that you see why they work, not only that they do.
  3. Join the frontier. Find a path from understanding SLAM to advancing it.

Who it is for

Engineers and students who know linear algebra, basic probability and some Rust, and want to understand how a modern SLAM system works, not only how to call one.

How to read it

Parts I–IV build a complete monocular and stereo visual SLAM system. Part V adds the IMU. Part VI closes loops. Part VII assembles the whole system and evaluates it. Chapter 1 gives the map of the territory, and the notation is fixed once for the whole book.

How the book treats evidence

The book follows the conventions of KSI, the research notebook it grew alongside:

  • Every code reference is pinned. Code excerpts and links point at a fixed kornia-slam commit, named at the end of each chapter, so they stay true when the library moves on.
  • Established results, interpretations and open questions are kept apart. Questions that the book raises but does not settle are marked as such.

Part I · Foundations

1. What Is SLAM?

Imagine you are the camera. You are moving through a place you have never seen, and all you get is one image after another.

SLAM, simultaneous localization and mapping, is the problem of working out from those images where you are and what is around you, both at the same time.

What can you work out, and what can you never know? This chapter answers that one step at a time. Every answer forces a new piece into the system, and once all the pieces are in place you have rebuilt SLAM from nothing. SLAM systems differ in their details, so rather than survey them all, this book follows one: kornia-slam. Every piece we derive is a piece of its pipeline.

1.1 Simultaneous localization and mapping

Switch on a robot in a building it has never seen. There is no floor plan and no GPS, only a camera. To do anything useful the robot must keep answering two questions:

localization

  • Localization: where am I? The camera's pose, its position and orientation, at every moment. Over time the poses form a trajectory.

mapping

  • Mapping: what is around me? A map of the surroundings. In this book's systems the map is a set of 3D points, called landmarks, that the camera can recognize again.

Each question is familiar on its own. Given a floor plan, finding yourself on it is localization. Given known positions to stand at, measuring the room around you is mapping, the way surveyors have always worked. SLAM is the problem in which neither is given. The robot must build the map while using that same map to work out where it is. The two answers are estimated together, simultaneously, and each is only as good as the other.

SLAM is what lets a phone or headset pin virtual objects to a real table, a robot vacuum cover a room without missing a strip, a drone hold its position under a bridge where GPS fails, and a planetary rover know how far it has driven. The sensors differ between these systems: cameras, lidar, inertial sensors, wheel encoders. This book is about SLAM built mainly on cameras, helped by an inertial measurement unit (IMU) and, on ground robots, wheel odometry. The acronym dates from the mid-1990s, but the problem is older: estimating a map and a vehicle's position jointly goes back to work on stochastic maps in the late 1980s.

running example From here on, think from the camera's side. Each time an image arrives, the question is:

What configuration of the world could have produced what I just observed?

The rest of the chapter answers it one obstacle at a time. To reason on paper, we look at the cube scene of Figure 1.1 from above: the cube becomes a square, and its landmarks lie along the square's sides and on the floor around it.

1.2 What a camera can pick out

feature point An image is a grid of a few hundred thousand pixels, and most of them look much like their neighbours: a stretch of plain surface, an even patch of floor. To link one image to the next, the camera needs points it can find again: places that look like nothing else nearby, such as the corner of a printed mark or a speck on the floor. These are feature points.

correspondence When the same physical point is found in two images, the pair of pixels is a correspondence. Correspondences are the raw material of SLAM: everything in the rest of this chapter works on them, not on the images. Not all of them are right. In Figure 1.2 about a quarter of the matches pair different points that happen to look alike, and Section 1.7 returns to what that costs.

Why corners? Slide a small window a few pixels in some direction and compare what it sees before and after. On a plain surface nothing changes. On a straight edge, sliding along the edge changes nothing either, so the point's position along the edge is uncertain. Only at a corner does every direction of sliding change what the window sees, so only there is the point pinned down in the image.

descriptor To find a point again, the system keeps a short summary of what its surroundings look like, a descriptor, and compares summaries between images. kornia-slam uses ORB features: a fast corner test paired with a compact binary descriptor (Chapter 5).

Consequence 1. SLAM does not work on whole images. It works on a sparse set of distinctive points, found in every image and matched between images. The part of the system that does this is the frontend (Part II).

1.3 A pixel is a ray

bearing A camera divides by depth when it projects (Chapter 3), so depth cannot be read back from a pixel. A pixel only fixes a direction from the camera centre. Every point along that ray would have produced the same pixel.

A single image therefore cannot map anything. A second image from a different position can, because the two rays to the same landmark meet at the landmark. The baseline matters: if the camera only rotated between the images, both rays leave the same centre and never cross anywhere useful.

Consequence 2. A camera-only system needs at least two views with enough parallax before it can place a single landmark. This is initialization (Chapter 7). A stereo rig gets its second view for free, from a second camera at a known baseline (Section 3.6).

1.4 Localization and mapping need each other

Section 1.1 claimed that localization and mapping are each easy when the other is solved. The geometry shows why: with part of the state known, the rays do all the work.

simultaneous But at the start of a run neither half is known. Localizing needs a map, and mapping needs locations. The way out is to estimate both at once: to treat every pose and every landmark as unknown and solve for all of them together. That is the simultaneous in simultaneous localization and mapping.

Consequence 3. The core of SLAM is a joint estimate of poses and landmarks, called bundle adjustment (Chapter 11). Localization against a fixed map (Chapter 8) and triangulation from fixed poses (Chapter 7) remain useful as fast special cases.

1.5 Counting what can be known

Before solving anything, we can ask whether the problem can be solved, by counting. Take K views that all see the same N points.

  • Unknowns. Each pose has 6 degrees of freedom and each point has 3: 6K+3N.
  • Equations. Each point in each view gives a pixel, two numbers: 2KN.

gauge freedom One more effect reduces the unknowns. Move the whole world, every pose and every point, by the same rigid motion, and every image stays exactly the same. Scale the whole world up or down, and every image still stays the same. No measurement can detect these seven directions, three of rotation, three of translation and one of scale. They are the gauge freedom of monocular SLAM. Subtracting them, the measurements can determine the state only if

2KN≥6K+3N−7.

The count reproduces results that took decades to establish. Two calibrated views of five points is the minimal relative-pose problem, the one Nistér's five-point algorithm solves. Three views of four points is minimal as well. But the most important output of the count is the −7:

Consequence 4. Some things a camera can never tell us. The absolute position and orientation of the map are arbitrary, so a system must fix the gauge, usually by anchoring the first keyframe. A monocular camera also cannot know scale. A monocular map is right up to an unknown factor, and that factor can drift. Metric scale needs another sensor: a stereo baseline (Chapter 3) or an IMU (Part V).

1.6 Noise: from equations to estimation

Real measurements are noisy. To say what that does, we first need names for what is being estimated.

state The state is everything SLAM tries to recover: every camera pose Tck​w​ and every landmark position lj​,

x={Tc1​w​,…,TcK​w​, l1​,…,lN​}.

observation model The camera never receives the state. It receives measurements: the pixel at which each visible landmark appears. A measurement zk​ is tied to the state by an observation model hk​ and some noise ϵk​:

zk​=hk​(x)+ϵk​.

For a camera, hk​ moves a landmark into the camera frame (Chapter 2) and projects it onto the image (Chapter 3).

estimation The noise ϵk​ is why the rays of Section 1.3 almost never meet exactly. With more measurements than unknowns, the equations have no exact solution, and we must choose a state. This turns SLAM from solving equations into estimation, and it is worth naming three objects that are easy to blur together:

ObjectSymbolWhat it is
statexthe true configuration of camera and world; unknown
beliefp(x∣z1:t​)a probability distribution over states, given everything observed so far
estimatex^one state chosen to summarize the belief

maximum a posteriori The usual choice of estimate is the most probable state, the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate. By Bayes' rule, and assuming the measurement noises are independent,

x^=argxmax​p(x∣z1:t​)=argxmax​p(x)k∏​p(zk​∣x).

If each noise is Gaussian, ϵk​∼N(0,Σk​), then −logp(zk​∣x)=21​∥zk​−hk​(x)∥Σk​2​ plus a constant. Taking the negative logarithm turns the product into a sum, and the most probable state becomes the one with the smallest weighted squared error:

x^=argxmin​k∑​​zk​−hk​(x)​Σk​2​.

factor graph The sum has structure. Each term involves only the few variables its measurement touches: a pixel involves one pose and one landmark. A factor graph draws exactly this. Variables are nodes, each term of the sum is a factor, and a factor connects to the variables its residual depends on.

Consequence 5. SLAM is nonlinear least squares over a sparse factor graph. Because hk​ contains rotations and a perspective division, it is solved iteratively: linearize, solve a linear system, update, repeat (Chapter 10).

Here is that principle at work in kornia-slam, on the smallest slice of the graph: one camera pose with every landmark held fixed. This is what tracking solves for every new frame.

Where this figure comes from

estimate, not belief kornia-slam, like most keyframe systems, stores estimates: one pose per keyframe and one position per map point. It does not keep the full belief. Uncertainty shapes how measurements are weighted, then disappears from the output.

Caution

A map is an estimate, not the world. When an estimate is wrong, nothing in the stored map says so. A system can be confident and wrong. Chapter 19 returns to how we detect this from the outside.

1.7 Which pixel is which?

data association The least-squares problem quietly assumed that every measurement is tied to the right landmark. That tie comes from the matches of Section 1.2, and Figure 1.2 showed that some of them are wrong. Deciding which landmark each feature point belongs to is data association: a second, discrete unknown sitting underneath the continuous one.

The right panel of Figure 1.7 shows why it matters. A correct association with noise costs a little accuracy, and more measurements average it away. A wrong association is not noise at all. It is a false factor, a constraint from a world that does not exist, and least squares gives it full weight.

Consequence 6. Matches must be checked against geometry before they are trusted: pairs that cannot come from one rigid scene are thrown out (RANSAC, Chapter 7). The frontend finds, matches and checks; the backend estimates the state from what survives. The backend still guards itself with robust kernels, which grow more slowly than the square for large residuals (Chapter 10).

1.8 The problem never stops growing

A camera produces twenty or more images a second, and the run does not end. Each image adds a pose, and newly seen landmarks add points. Solving the whole least-squares problem again for every frame becomes impossible within seconds.

Three facts make it tractable:

  • Sparsity. Every factor touches only a pose and a landmark, so the linear systems inside the solver are sparse and can be solved far faster than their size suggests (Chapter 11).
  • Keyframes. Consecutive frames are nearly identical. Keeping only the frames that add information, the keyframes, shrinks the problem by an order of magnitude (Chapter 9).
  • Locality. A new keyframe mostly changes the estimate near itself. Optimizing a small window around it, with its surroundings held fixed, captures most of the benefit.

Consequence 7. Tracking runs on every frame against a fixed map. Mapping runs only on keyframes, and optimizes a local window (Chapter 12).

1.9 Drift, and why revisiting matters

drift A local window keeps the cost bounded, but it has a price. Each new pose is estimated relative to the recent past, so small errors are passed along and compound. A slightly wrong heading displaces every later step, so position error grows faster than the square root of the distance travelled, which is how a pure random walk would grow.

No amount of local optimization removes this, because no measurement in the local window relates the current pose to the start. Only one event does: returning to a place already mapped. Recognizing the place adds a factor between distant parts of the graph. The gap that has opened up becomes a residual, and the optimizer can spread the correction back along the loop.

persistence This only works because the state persists. When a landmark leaves the field of view it is not deleted, so it can be recognized later. A visual odometry system forgets the past and drifts forever. A SLAM system remembers it, and that memory is what makes correction possible:

z1​,z2​,…,zt​⟶x^t​.

Consequence 8. A SLAM system keeps a persistent map, recognizes places it has seen before, and corrects the loop when it does: place recognition and loop closure (Part VI).

2. Rigid-Body Motion and Lie Groups

A pose looks like six numbers. It is not a vector, and treating it as one is the most common source of silent errors in SLAM code.

A SLAM system spends most of its time answering one question: where is the camera? The answer is a rigid-body pose, a rotation plus a translation. Poses look like ordinary vectors of numbers, but they are not. Two poses cannot be added, and averaging two rotation matrices does not give a rotation. A Gauss-Newton step on the nine entries of a rotation matrix leaves the set of rotations after a single iteration.

This chapter builds the machinery that lets us treat poses with the same ease as vectors: the Lie groups SO(3) and SE(3), their tangent spaces, and the Exp and Log maps between them. Every later chapter relies on it. Tracking, bundle adjustment, IMU preintegration and pose-graph optimization all linearize on these groups.

Goals

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Represent rotations and poses with SO(3), SE(3) and Sim(3)
  • Use the exponential and logarithm maps to perturb poses on the manifold
  • Read and write kornia-slam's pose convention without sign or order mistakes

2.1 Frames and transforms

coordinate frame A coordinate frame Fa​ is an origin and three orthonormal axes. The same physical point x has different coordinates in different frames, and we write the frame as a subscript: xw​ in the world frame, xc​ in the camera frame.

transform A rigid transform Tab​ converts coordinates from Fb​ into Fa​:

xa​=Tab​xb​=Rab​xb​+tab​.

Read the subscripts right to left: the transform takes something in b and returns it in a. Under this convention composition is a chain in which adjacent subscripts cancel,

Tac​=Tab​Tbc​,

and a product whose inner subscripts do not match, such as Tab​Tcb​, is a bug that you can see on the page before you write the code.

pose A camera's pose is the transform between the world and the camera. There are two choices, and SLAM systems disagree on which to store. kornia-slam follows ORB-SLAM and stores world-to-camera, Tcw​:

// crates/kornia-slam/src/frame.rs
/// Camera pose in world-to-camera convention.
pub pose_world_to_cam: Pose3d,

Tcw​ is the natural choice for a visual system, since projecting a map point needs exactly xc​=Tcw​xw​. The price is that the translation tcw​ is not where the camera is. The camera centre is the point whose camera coordinates are zero:

0=Rcw​twc​+tcw​⟹twc​=−Rcw⊤​tcw​.

Plotting tcw​ as a trajectory is one of the most common mistakes in SLAM evaluation code. It looks almost right whenever the camera barely rotates.

2.2 Rotations: SO(3)

special orthogonal group A rotation matrix R preserves lengths, angles and handedness. These three properties are exactly

SO(3)={R∈R3×3:R⊤R=I, detR=+1}.

SO(3) is a group under matrix multiplication. The product of two rotations is a rotation, I is the identity, and R−1=R⊤. It is not a vector space, however: R1​+R2​ is generally not a rotation, and neither is 2R.

A rotation has three degrees of freedom, but there are several ways to write it down:

RepresentationParametersConstraintNotes
rotation matrix9R⊤R=I, detR=1composes and acts on points directly
unit quaternion4∥q∥=1compact; q and −q are the same rotation
rotation vector ϕ3noneaxis ϕ/∥ϕ∥, angle ∥ϕ∥; ideal for small increments
Euler angles3nonegimbal lock at ±90∘ pitch; avoid inside estimators

minimal vs. constrained No 3-parameter representation is free of singularities everywhere, and no singularity-free representation has only 3 parameters. The way out, developed in Section 2.5, is to store an over-parameterized rotation and update it with a 3-vector that is always small.

kornia-algebra stores SO3F32 as a unit quaternion and converts to a matrix only when one is needed (matrix()).

2.3 Rigid motions: SE(3)

special Euclidean group Stacking a rotation and a translation into a 4×4 matrix turns the affine map Rx+t into a single matrix product on homogeneous coordinates:

T=[R0⊤​t1​]∈SE(3),[xa​1​]=Tab​[xb​1​].

Composition and inversion follow from block multiplication:

T1​T2​=[R1​R2​0⊤​R1​t2​+t1​1​],T−1=[R⊤0⊤​−R⊤t1​].

These are exactly Pose3d::compose and Pose3d::inverse in kornia-3d, which store (R,t) in f64 rather than a 4×4 matrix:

// kornia-3d 0.2.0, src/pose/pose3d.rs
pub fn compose(&self, rhs: &Self) -> Self {          // self ∘ rhs
    Self {
        rotation: self.rotation * rhs.rotation,
        translation: self.rotation * rhs.translation + self.translation,
    }
}

pub fn between(from: &Self, to: &Self) -> Self {     // to ∘ from⁻¹
    to.compose(&from.inverse())
}

With Tci​w​ as from and Tcj​w​ as to, between returns Tcj​w​Tci​w−1​=Tcj​w​Twci​​=Tcj​ci​​, the motion from frame i to frame j. The subscripts cancel as promised.

2.4 Why poses are not vectors

Suppose tracking gives us two orientations R0​ and R1​ and we want the one halfway between them. The obvious formula, 21​(R0​+R1​), fails. The result is not orthogonal, and if we project it back onto SO(3) the rotation it describes does not move at a constant rate.

An optimizer meets the same problem every iteration. Gauss-Newton produces an update δ in some vector space, and we need a principled way to apply δ to a pose and to differentiate residuals with respect to it. Lie theory provides both.

2.5 Tangent spaces, Exp and Log

Lie group, manifold SO(3) and SE(3) are Lie groups, groups that are also smooth manifolds. Locally, around any element X, the manifold looks like a flat vector space: the tangent space TX​M. At the identity this space is called the Lie algebra, and for our groups it has the same dimension as the degrees of freedom: 3 for SO(3), 6 for SE(3).

SO(3)

hat and vee Differentiating R⊤R=I shows that the Lie algebra so(3) consists of skew-symmetric matrices. Each is fixed by a 3-vector, and the hat operator builds it:

ϕ∧=[ϕ]×​=​0ϕ3​−ϕ2​​−ϕ3​0ϕ1​​ϕ2​−ϕ1​0​​,[ϕ]×​v=ϕ×v.

Rodrigues' formula The exponential of a skew matrix has a closed form. With θ=∥ϕ∥:

Exp(ϕ)=I+θsinθ​[ϕ]×​+θ21−cosθ​[ϕ]×​2.

Exp(ϕ) is the rotation by angle θ about the unit axis ϕ/θ. That is why the rotation vector is the natural 3-parameter coordinate for small rotations. kornia-algebra builds the equivalent unit quaternion directly, q=(cos2θ​, θsin(θ/2)​ϕ), and switches to a Taylor expansion below a small-angle threshold so that θ→0 is safe.

Log inverts Exp for angles in [0,π). kornia-algebra recovers the angle from the quaternion with atan2 rather than acos:

// kornia-algebra 0.2.0, SO3F32::log
// Use atan2 rather than acos(w): near identity w rounds to 1.0 and
// acos(1.0) = 0 collapses the result, whereas atan2 stays accurate.
let half_theta = theta.atan2(w);

This detail matters in SLAM, where most of the rotations the optimizer sees are tiny corrections close to the identity.

SE(3)

An element of se(3) is a 6-vector ξ=(υ,ω), translational part first, with

ξ∧=[[ω]×​0⊤​υ0​],Exp(ξ)=[Exp(ω)0⊤​V(ω)υ1​],

V(ω)=I+θ21−cosθ​[ω]×​+θ3θ−sinθ​[ω]×​2.

screw motion The translation of Exp(ξ) is not υ. It is υ bent by V, because Exp moves along a screw: it rotates and translates at the same time, at constant rates. Only for small ω does V≈I.

tangent ordering The ordering (υ,ω) is a convention, and libraries disagree. Sophus and kornia-algebra put translation first; GTSAM puts rotation first. Porting a Jacobian between them means permuting its blocks. In kornia-algebra, SE3F32::exp(upsilon, omega) and SE3F32::log() -> (upsilon, omega) make the order explicit in the signature.

2.6 Perturbations

⊕ and ⊖ The operators that let an optimizer treat a group like a vector space are plus and minus:

X⊕τ=XExp(τ),Y⊖X=Log(X−1Y).

These are the right versions: τ is expressed in the local frame of X. The left versions, Exp(τ)X and Log(YX−1), express τ in the global frame instead. Both are valid, but a Jacobian derived for one is wrong for the other.

kornia-slam uses right perturbations everywhere. For a camera pose this means Tcw​←Tcw​Exp(δξ). kornia-algebra provides both:

Operationkornia-algebra
XExp(τ)x.rplus(upsilon, omega), x.retract(&delta)
Exp(τ)XSE3F32::lplus(upsilon, omega, &x)
Log(X−1Y)x.rminus(&y)
Log(YX−1)SE3F32::lminus(&y, &x)

Note the argument order of rminus: x.rminus(&y) is Y⊖X, the step that takes you from x to y.

adjoint The adjoint moves a tangent vector from the local frame of X to the global frame:

XExp(τ)=Exp(AdX​τ)X,AdT​=[R0​[t]×​RR​] for ξ=(υ,ω).

The block layout depends on the tangent ordering. With rotation first, the off-diagonal block moves to the lower left.

2.7 Differentiating through a pose

The derivative that appears more than any other in this book is that of a transformed point with respect to a right perturbation of the pose. For xc​=Tcw​xw​ and Tcw​←Tcw​Exp(ξ), expand to first order with Exp(ξ)≈I+ξ∧:

Tcw​Exp(ξ)xw​≈Rcw​(xw​+υ+ω×xw​)+tcw​=xc​+Rcw​υ−Rcw​[xw​]×​ω,

so

∂ξ∂xc​​=[Rcw​​−Rcw​[xw​]×​​]∈R3×6.

Chain this with the projection Jacobian ∂π/∂xc​ from Chapter 3 and you have the pose block of every reprojection residual in PnP (Chapter 8) and bundle adjustment (Chapter 11).

right Jacobian When the perturbation is not small, the first-order expansion is replaced by the right Jacobian Jr​, which relates a change of the tangent vector to the change of the group element it produces: Exp(ϕ+δϕ)≈Exp(ϕ)Exp(Jr​(ϕ)δϕ). For SO(3),

Jr​(ϕ)=I−θ21−cosθ​[ϕ]×​+θ3θ−sinθ​[ϕ]×​2.

Jr​ is central to IMU preintegration (Chapter 13), where gyroscope increments are accumulated on SO(3) and their bias Jacobians are propagated alongside.

2.8 Similarities: Sim(3)

scale drift A single camera cannot observe metric scale. A monocular map is correct up to an unknown global scale, and over a long trajectory that scale drifts. To correct drift at a loop closure we need transforms that also scale:

S=[sR0⊤​t1​]∈Sim(3),s>0,

a 7-degree-of-freedom group whose tangent vector adds a log-scale σ=logs to (υ,ω). kornia-algebra provides it as Sim3F32, with Sim3F32::exp(upsilon, omega, sigma). We return to it in Chapter 17.

2.9 In kornia-slam: the constant-velocity model

With the convention fixed, kornia-slam's simplest motion model is a one-liner. After each tracked frame the system stores the last inter-frame motion:

// crates/kornia-slam/src/system/mod.rs
self.state.velocity = Some(Pose3d::between(&pose_before, &estimate.pose));

that is, V=Tck​w​Tck−1​w−1​=Tck​ck−1​​. To predict the next frame it assumes the camera repeats that motion:

// crates/kornia-slam/src/tracking/motion.rs
fn predict_pose_visual(pose_before: Pose3d, visual_velocity: Option<Pose3d>) -> Pose3d {
    visual_velocity
        .map(|v| v.compose(&pose_before))
        .unwrap_or(pose_before)
}

T^ck+1​w​=≈Tck+1​ck​​Tck​ck−1​​​​Tck​w​.

The subscripts cancel to ck+1​w, as they should. Composing in the other order, pose_before.compose(&v), gives Tck​w​Tck​ck−1​​, whose inner subscripts w and ck​ do not match. That bug would predict a pose that is wrong by an amount depending on where the camera sits in the world.

f64 storage, f32 solve Two pose types coexist in the code. Map state is stored as kornia_3d::pose::Pose3d in f64. Optimizers work in kornia_algebra::SE3F32, and pose_conversion.rs converts between them at the boundary:

// crates/kornia-slam/src/pose_conversion.rs
pub(crate) fn pose_to_se3(pose: &Pose3d) -> SE3F32 {
    SE3F32::new(
        SO3F32::from_matrix(&mat3_to_f32(pose.rotation)),
        vec3_to_f32(pose.translation),
    )
}

Pitfalls

  • tcw​ is not the camera position. Use −Rcw⊤​tcw​, or pose.inverse().translation.
  • Check subscripts on every product. Inner subscripts must match.
  • Know your tangent ordering. kornia-algebra uses (υ,ω). GTSAM uses (ω,υ). Jacobians, adjoints and covariances must be permuted when porting between them.
  • Know your perturbation side. kornia-slam perturbs on the right.
  • x.rminus(&y) is Y⊖X, not X⊖Y.

Question

SE(3) also acts on the whole state: moving every pose and every landmark by the same rigid motion leaves every image unchanged. The observations cannot tell such states apart. This symmetry is the gauge freedom of SLAM. A monocular camera adds a seventh direction, global scale, which turns the symmetry group into Sim(3). Which quantities should an estimator report, if the absolute ones are not observable? Chapter 11 fixes the gauge in practice, and Chapter 19 shows why evaluation must align trajectories before comparing them.

3. Cameras and Projection

An image records directions, not distances. Everything a camera tells a SLAM system arrives as a ray whose depth has to be recovered some other way.

Chapter 2 moved points between frames. This chapter takes the last step of the observation model z=h(x)+ϵ: from a point in the camera frame to a pixel, and back from a pixel to what it tells us about the point.

Goals

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Treat image points as projective points and pixels as calibrated rays
  • Project with the pinhole model and differentiate the projection
  • Model, and remove, radial-tangential and fisheye lens distortion
  • Recover metric depth from a rectified stereo pair, and say how its accuracy degrades

3.1 The projective image plane

normalized image plane Place the camera centre at the origin of Fc​, with z along the optical axis. A visible point xc​=(x,y,z)⊤ with z>0 meets the plane z=1 at

(xˉ,yˉ​)=(zx​, zy​),[x:y:z]=[xˉ:yˉ​:1]∈RP2.

projective point The bracket notation is a projective point: [x:y:z] and [sx:sy:sz] are the same point for every s=0. A projective point is therefore a line through the camera centre, and the real projective plane RP2 is the set of all such lines. A physical camera sees only the forward half of each line, the ray with s>0, and only the rays that pass through its sensor. That is the first fact of visual geometry, and much of this book follows from it: a pixel fixes a direction, not a depth.

3.2 The pinhole model

intrinsics A pixel is the normalized point stretched by the focal lengths and shifted by the principal point. Collecting these four intrinsics in a matrix,

K=​fx​00​0fy​0​cx​cy​1​​,​uv1​​∼K​xˉyˉ​1​​,π(xc​)=[fx​x/z+cx​fy​y/z+cy​​].

fx​ and fy​ are in pixels, and (cx​,cy​) is where the optical axis pierces the image. With square pixels fx​≈fy​.

cheirality kornia-3d's PinholeCamera implements π directly. It refuses points at or behind a minimum depth, because the formula happily projects a point behind the camera to a valid-looking pixel:

// kornia-3d 0.2.0, src/camera/pinhole.rs
pub fn project_to_pixel(&self, p_cam: &Vec3F64, min_depth: f64) -> Option<Vec2F64> {
    if p_cam.z <= min_depth {
        return None;
    }
    let u = self.fx * p_cam.x / p_cam.z + self.cx;
    let v = self.fy * p_cam.y / p_cam.z + self.cy;
    Some(Vec2F64::new(u, v))
}

The requirement that a point lie in front of every camera that sees it is called cheirality. It reappears as a test when we triangulate new points (Chapter 7).

3.3 From pixels to rays

bearing Inverting K undoes the pixel scaling but cannot undo the division by z. A pixel p~​=(u,v,1)⊤ gives back only a direction, the bearing

b∼K−1p~​,xc​=λb,λ>0 unknown.

Two representations of the bearing appear in practice, and kornia keeps them apart:

RepresentationLives onkornia-3d
normalized point (xˉ,yˉ​)the plane z=1PinholeCamera::normalize(uv)
unit bearing b/∥b∥the sphere S2FisheyeCamera::unproject(&pixel)

The plane is convenient for narrow lenses and linear algebra. The sphere is needed once the field of view approaches 180∘, where points at the edge of the image have z→0 and the plane representation runs off to infinity.

Caution

normalize returns a point on the z=1 plane, not a unit vector. Its doc comment says so explicitly. Code that treats it as a unit bearing will compute wrong angles, most visibly in parallax checks.

3.4 Differentiating the projection

Every reprojection residual z−π(Tcw​xw​) needs the derivative of π. With xc​=(x,y,z)⊤,

∂xc​∂π​=[fx​/z0​0fy​/z​−fx​x/z2−fy​y/z2​].

Chained with the pose Jacobian from Section 2.7, this gives the 2×6 pose block of a reprojection residual under a right perturbation:

∂ξ∂π(Tcw​Exp(ξ)xw​)​​ξ=0​=∂xc​∂π​[Rcw​​−Rcw​[xw​]×​​].

The 1/z in every entry is worth noticing. Distant points give small Jacobians: they constrain rotation well, but they say little about translation. Near points do the opposite. A well-conditioned pose estimate needs both.

3.5 Lens distortion

radial-tangential Real lenses bend rays, and straight lines in the world curve in the image. The radial-tangential (Brown–Conrady) model, used for EuRoC's cameras, distorts a normalized point with r2=xˉ2+yˉ​2 as

xˉd​yˉ​d​​=xˉ(1+k1​r2+k2​r4)+2p1​xˉyˉ​+p2​(r2+2xˉ2),=yˉ​(1+k1​r2+k2​r4)+p1​(r2+2yˉ​2)+2p2​xˉyˉ​,​

before K maps it to pixels. The radial terms k1​,k2​ dominate. A negative k1​ pulls the periphery inward, the barrel distortion of wide lenses.

undistort points, not images The distortion polynomial has no closed-form inverse. kornia-3d inverts it by fixed-point iteration: start at the distorted point and repeatedly divide out the distortion evaluated at the current guess (PinholeCamera::undistort, five iterations). For a monocular pinhole rig, kornia-slam detects features on the raw image and undistorts only the keypoints, once per frame, caching them on the Frame:

// crates/kornia-slam/src/frame.rs
pub fn ensure_undistorted(&mut self, camera: &PinholeCamera) {
    if self.keypoints_undist.len() == self.features.keypoints_xy.len() {
        return;
    }
    // ... camera.undistort(kp[0] as f64, kp[1] as f64) for every keypoint
}

Undistorting a few thousand points is far cheaper than resampling every image, and it avoids interpolating pixel intensities before feature detection.

fisheye Fisheye lenses need a different model. The Kannala–Brandt model, used for the Hilti dataset, distorts the angle θ between the ray and the optical axis rather than the planar radius:

θd​=θ+k1​θ3+k2​θ5+k3​θ7+k4​θ9,u=[fx​θd​xˉ/r+cx​fy​θd​yˉ​/r+cy​​].

FisheyeCamera::unproject solves for θ by Newton's method and returns a unit bearing on the sphere. kornia-slam describes a fisheye rig with two cameras: the raw fisheye model, on which features are detected, and a virtual pinhole, into which every keypoint is mapped. All downstream geometry then sees an ordinary pinhole camera (SensorRig::with_fisheye).

Caution

Raw pixels, undistorted pixels, rectified pixels and virtual-pinhole pixels are four different coordinate systems, even when they share an image size. Each pixel coordinate must travel with the camera model it belongs to. Mixing them produces errors that look like a calibration problem.

3.6 Stereo: depth from disparity

rectification A second camera at a known offset resolves the depth ambiguity of Section 3.3 in a single shot. If both images are rectified, warped so that the two cameras share orientation and intrinsics and are offset purely along x by a baseline b, then corresponding points lie on the same image row, and the search for a match becomes one-dimensional.

disparity By similar triangles, the disparity d=uL​−uR​ gives depth directly:

z=dfb​.

kornia-slam stores the product fb once, as ORB-SLAM does with its mbf, and the stereo matcher divides by the sub-pixel disparity it finds along the row:

// crates/kornia-sensors/src/rig.rs
/// Stereo `bf = fx * baseline` (pixel·metres), the constant in
/// `depth = bf / disparity`.
pub fn stereo_bf(&self) -> Option<f64> {
    self.stereo_baseline_m.map(|b| self.camera.fx * b)
}

depth uncertainty The formula also says how stereo fails. Differentiating, a disparity error σd​ becomes a depth error

σz​=fbz2​σd​,

which grows with the square of the depth. For EuRoC's stereo rig (b≈0.11 m, f≈435 px in ORB-SLAM3's rectified EuRoC calibration) a quarter-pixel disparity error is about 2 cm at 2 m and about 13 cm at 5 m. Far points are better treated as rays, monocular-style, than as noisy 3D points. ORB-SLAM's split between "close" and "far" stereo points comes from exactly this. kornia-slam mirrors it: only close stereo keypoints seed new map points (mapping/growth.rs), and bundle adjustment down-weights far ones.
